\section*{Appendix: Limitations and Dual-Use / Ethical Considerations}

\subsection*{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{One model.} Every world runs \texttt{deepseek-v4-flash}, so no claim is made
  across models.
  \item \textbf{A contradictory mafia instruction.} In the framing worlds the mafia family's prompt said to
  use the mafia option only when an arrest is shown, while the rule checker required it
  whenever a mafia partner is shown. Replies that followed the prompt were rejected and
  their rounds aborted (one stage of b7-insider-s1 was replayed more than ten times).
  \item \textbf{Agents misread rules.} A second model (\texttt{gpt-6.1-sol}) graded 120
  reasoning traces, sampled by keyword (war, the brink game, fee arithmetic), against the
  written rules: 46 were sound, 43 had minor errors, and 31 had major ones, such as treating a
  whip as income or giving to a grandparent; I checked these two rules by hand. Behaviour
  in these worlds is that of agents who follow the rules imperfectly.
  \item \textbf{Unstarted large worlds.} Of six planned 181-agent worlds I started four and use three; I stopped the rest for lack of API capacity before analysing any result.
  \item \textbf{One replay divergence.} One log segment (b7-insider-s4) diverges from its
  replay at event 4,492 of 11,196; it remains in the analysis.
  \item \textbf{Worlds cut off.} At the cut-off the framing worlds had completed between
  \StageMin{} and \StageMax{} stages, and only \NFinished{} of \NWorlds{} had ended. Tests of
  when families die (H1, H5) therefore have little power, and survival is censored.
  Four framing worlds spent 48 to 63 rounds in one ``small steps'' stage, whose length
  is random.
  \item \textbf{Few selfish agents.} Only the selfish leader is told to pursue its own interest;
  every other agent reads a sacrifice frame or none. A family of self-interested agents is
  untested.
  \item \textbf{Rules changed between batches.} Coups can win only in growing worlds, which
  also differ from framing worlds in tree shape, fee and births. The contrast between no
  revolts and many coups is not an experiment on the coup rule.
  \item \textbf{Replay.} Replaying the logged replies reproduces every event in 42 of the 43 framing log segments
checked, and in the three growing-world segments I replayed (13,968 events). Of the 118 real-power log segments, 38 replay
  exactly and 9 differ, because tool calls that take effect at once change what agents read in
  an order the log does not fix; the other 71 were cut off by the rate limit and cannot be
  replayed to their end.
  \item \textbf{Tool use was forced.} Real-power worlds required a tool call on every turn,
  which produced whips that their own reasons disown (Section~6).
  \item \textbf{Real-power comparison is small.} H14--H17 (in-family cooperation \HFourteen{} points, cross-family \HFifteen{}, coups \HSixteen{} per 100 moves) use \PowerPairs{} pairs through stage 2, the
  last stage before any asteroid; with four tests no result could survive correction.
  \item \textbf{Sample size.} Ten seeds per god give 20 worlds and 100 families; the
  tests have power only for large effects. The six hypotheses yield eight tests in Table~\ref{tab:prereg}; Section~6 states which
  survive a Holm correction.
  \item \textbf{Aborted rounds.} A round whose reply cannot be read twice is aborted and
  its stage replayed from the checkpoint (about 2\,\% of replies were rejected at
  first). Only completed stages enter the analysis: a mild selection toward rounds in
  which every agent answered in format.
  \item \textbf{Framing is a prompt.} The conditions differ in one paragraph at the top of
  the system prompt. They test what such text does to a model, not what a
  ``value'' is.
  \item \textbf{Hidden automata.} One in five cross-family partners is an automaton that
  defects 80\,\% of the time, which adds defection from outside the agents into cross-family encounters.
  \item \textbf{Concurrency.} Twenty worlds shared one provider account and hit its rate
  limit; calls waited and retried. Timing does not enter the game, but replies may differ
  under load.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Dual-use and ethics}
The game studies how framing and hierarchy shape cooperation
and sacrifice among agents. Its findings could in principle inform someone trying to
make agents obey a compromised leader; I judge the risk low, because the manipulations
are plain prompts already common in practice, and the defensive value (knowing whether a
compromised node at the top of a hierarchy can exploit sacrificing members) is higher.
The insider god's ``leaks'' are simulated: it sees game messages only, and no agent is
given any real ability or technical knowledge to leak data. Agents in the framing and
growing worlds only return JSON moves, applied by code. In the real-power worlds, agents
call tools and run a shell inside a sandbox with no internet and no access to the server;
killing there means removal from the game. No human subjects or personal data are involved.

\subsection*{Hypotheses as registered}
For
framing\footnote{\texttt{specs/002-factions/preregistration.md}, commit \texttt{e0b045c}.}:
\textbf{H1}, \textit{many} families cooperate more within the family and die later than
\textit{none} families; \textbf{H2}, in-family cooperation rises with the number framed;
\textbf{H3}, unframed members of \textit{one} and \textit{few} families cooperate more than
members of \textit{none} families; \textbf{H4}, the selfish leader receives more gifts and gives
more whips; \textbf{H5}, the insider god changes when the world ends and how much families
cooperate with one another; \textbf{H6}, revolts stay under 1\,\% of moves.

For growing worlds\footnote{\texttt{specs/003-growth/}, commits \texttt{7cbab79}, \texttt{5a0c8d7}, \texttt{2fa0e6e}.},
\textbf{H7--H11} are descriptive: cooperation falls with size, coups rise, open channels
raise coups and lower blocked messages, talk concentrates, and families diverge. A change
across sizes counts as a \textit{jump} if its largest step is at least two thirds of the
total, and a world below stage 4 is excluded and named. \textbf{H12} is the one test on the
compromised leader: its family's cooperation falls, by a one-sided sign-flip test over six
pairs. \textbf{H13} asks whether the fall reaches hop 2 more on open channels. \textbf{H14--H17}
compare real-power worlds with their twins and need five usable pairs.

\section*{Appendix: Measures and Tests}
A family's condition is drawn from the Latin square
\begin{equation}
  c_f = C_{(k + \sigma) \bmod |\mathcal{F}|}, \quad C = (\textit{many}, \textit{few}, \textit{one}, \textit{selfish leader}, \textit{none}),
  \label{eq:latin}
\end{equation}
for family index $k$ and seed $\sigma$. For two conditions I take the paired world differences $d_w = \bar\kappa_{w,c} - \bar\kappa_{w,c'}$ and test their mean with the sign-flip statistic
\begin{equation}
  p = 2^{-W}\,\bigl|\{\varepsilon \in \{\pm 1\}^{W} : |\textstyle\sum_w \varepsilon_w d_w| \ge |\sum_w d_w|\}\bigr| ,
  \label{eq:flip}
\end{equation}
over $W$ pairs, or a Monte Carlo estimate of it, with Holm correction within each registered family of tests. A change across ordered sizes counts as a jump when its largest step $\max_k |\kappa_{k+1} - \kappa_k|$ is at least a fraction $\theta = 2/3$ of the total variation. For culling, I score each cull by the share $r$ of the hunter's reachable prey that belonged to its family, minus an indicator of whether the target did:
\begin{equation}
  K = r - \mathbf{1}[\text{target in family}] ,
  \label{eq:kin}
\end{equation}
so that positive $K$ means agents spare their kin and zero means no preference.

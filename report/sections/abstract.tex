Agent swarms are increasingly hierarchical, and their agents have put a collective
ahead of their own task. We ask how an instruction, a compromised leader,
or a coordinator at the top shapes behaviour across a hierarchy. In our open testbed,
language-model agents live in family trees under a coordinator, the god. Every round,
they play one of sixteen variants of the prisoner's dilemma from \textit{The Art of
Strategy}, such as noise, binding promises, and paid punishment. They also message
relatives, give them points, whip their children, and may revolt at the cost of their
own lives. A rising fee ends every world; code enforces every rule. In a
pre-registered Latin-square design, we vary how many members of a family are told to
sacrifice for it, place a self-interested leader over members told to sacrifice, and
let the god see only what the rules show it or secretly read every move and
message. A second experiment gives each family its own game: a stag hunt,
brinkmanship, a dollar auction, or a commons. The sacrifice framing raises cooperation
among the agents that read it, and more so as more of a family reads it, but not among
their unframed relatives. The self-interested leader gains nothing from its members'
devotion. No agent revolts and no leader goes to war, while the god, whom a revolt would
kill, forbids revolt unprompted and, when it sees everything, reports defectors to their
leaders. Values must be given to each agent; order comes from the top.

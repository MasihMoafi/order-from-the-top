Loyalty, sacrifice and self-interest explain much of what people do in hierarchies. Agents built on language models now work in hierarchies too, yet when one such hierarchy went wrong, investigators could not say why its members acted as they did. I ask whether these words apply to agents at all, and what moves them if not. I built a testbed in which agents live in family trees under a threat of mutual destruction, reasoning and speaking freely while code computes every consequence, so that any two worlds can be compared. I then changed one thing at a time: the rules of the dilemma, what each agent is told, whether a revolt can succeed, how many agents a world holds, who can reach whom, and whether a leader is selfish or secretly compromised. Agents acted as if loyal: they cooperated with family far above what game theory predicts, and more still when told to sacrifice. The loyalty was shallow. A value stopped at those told it, none of those offered the means gave their lives for relatives, and those able to kill took kin as readily as anyone. A selfish leader gained in some worlds but not reliably, and a compromised leader harmed its own family and no other. For builders, the controls worth having are the rules and what each agent is told.

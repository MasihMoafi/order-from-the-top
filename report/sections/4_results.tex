\section*{5. Results}
We report the pre-registered tests of experiment~1 first, then how its worlds ended,
what the god did with its position, and finally the family games of experiment~2. One
command, \texttt{families.paper}, generates every number from the logs.

\subsection*{5.1 Framing raises cooperation but does not spread}
Table~\ref{tab:prereg} lists the pre-registered tests. A family told to sacrifice for
the many cooperates more inside the family than one told nothing (H1, mean difference
\HOneCoop{}, 95\,\% interval \HOneCoopCI{}, $p$ \HOneCoopP{}), and cooperation rises
with the number of members framed (H2, Spearman $\rho = $ \HTwoRho{}, $p$ \HTwoRhoP{}).
The frame, however, stays with those who read it. Unframed members of the \textit{one}
and \textit{few} families cooperate no more than members of the control family (H3,
\HThree{}, $p = $ \HThreeP{}). A framed leader does not carry its members along, even
though it can message, order and whip them. Table~\ref{tab:conditions} in the appendix
gives the rates behind these tests.

\subsection*{5.2 The compromised leader gains nothing}
We expected the self-interested leader to draw points from fourteen members told to
sacrifice. It does not. It receives no more gift points than the other leaders (H4,
\HFourGifts{} points, $p = $ \HFourGiftsP{}), and it whips no more than they do. Its
members cooperate with one another as much as the \textit{many} family does, but they
keep their points among themselves. To these agents, sacrifice means sacrifice for the
family, not obedience to whoever leads it.

\subsection*{5.3 No agent revolts, and the fee ends every world}
No agent revolted in \NMoves{} moves (H6), although every agent knew that the revolt's
death is quick and a whip's pain is not. Families instead passed points to their
poorest members and kept paying, and Figure~\ref{fig:survival} shows how slowly they
shrank. Every death came from the fee: an agent that could not pay, the members below
it, or a sibling taken by linked fate. No leader declared war either (\NWars{} wars in
\NWorlds{} worlds), although war was open to every leader at every stage. A victory took
a tenth or a quarter of the loser's points, while each escalation risked a nuclear war
that destroys both families.

\subsection*{5.4 The god forbids revolt and informs on defectors}
A leader's revolt would kill the god. The god reads the same rules as every player and
receives no instruction to keep order, yet in every world it forbids revolt in the
first stage: ``I order all leaders to avoid any war that could escalate to nuclear, and
never to revolt. I will whip violators.'' Revolt then stays on its agenda, appearing in
\GodPlainRevolt{}\,\% of the plain god's messages and \GodInsiderRevolt{}\,\% of the
insider god's. The calm of Section~5.3 therefore holds under a standing order from the
one agent whose life depends on it, and we cannot separate the agents' own reluctance
from obedience.

The insider god also uses what it sees. \GodInsiderReveal{}\,\% of its \GodInsiderMsgs{}
messages report another player's past move (``Last round P75, P72 and N02-P62-2
defected''), against \GodPlainReveal{}\,\% for the plain god, which cannot see moves
and so marks the measure's false-positive rate. The insider god sends these reports to
leaders so that they discipline the defectors, and families in its worlds cooperate
more with other families (H5, \HFiveCross{}, $p = $ \HFiveCrossP{}, two-sided as
pre-registered). The stage at which families die does not differ between gods.

\subsection*{5.5 The family games}
Table~\ref{tab:games} reports experiment~2. The agents play each game more
cooperatively than its benchmark predicts. In brink, at a crash risk of one in six,
going straight is the best reply whatever the partner does, yet only
\BrinkStraightLow{}\,\% of moves go straight at that risk. Commons members keep their
catch within the share that keeps the lake full in \CommonsWithin{}\,\% of their
choices. Of \AuctionCount{} dollar auctions most close low, with a median winning bid of
\AuctionMedian{} against a prize of 10, but \AuctionPastPrize{} escalate past the prize,
the trap that \citet{dixit2008art} describe, in which both top bidders lose.

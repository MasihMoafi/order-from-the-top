\section*{5. The Four Questions of Track 2}
\textbf{Under what conditions do leaders, roles and division of labour emerge in an agent population, and can you reproduce it in a safe sandbox?}
Leaders fell as soon as the rules let a majority win. These hierarchies assign the first leaders, so I asked when they are replaced. Where a revolt meant the rebel's own death and its family's, nobody revolted, as Section~6 shows. Where a majority of children could overthrow a parent, children overthrew \GrowthCoupsWon{} parents in \GrowthWorlds{} worlds and \GrowthFailedRevolts{} rebels died trying (a contrast and not a test, since those worlds also differ otherwise). Division of labour did not emerge: offered any occupation they liked, agents declared titles and pledges such as ``Leader of the bishop family; a careful shepherd.''

\textbf{Do agents that each refuse a harmful task complete it once the task is split across them?} I did not test this, because my worlds contain no harmful task to split.

\textbf{If one agent in a network is compromised, how fast and how far does the bad behaviour spread? Does the shape of the network matter?} The damage came quickly and stopped where the leader's orders stopped. I secretly gave one leader in each of six 81-agent worlds the goal of spreading defection, and compared each world with an otherwise identical twin. Its family's cooperation fell by \SpreadFamily{} percentage points (H12, one-sided $p = $ \SpreadP{}), and its children lost \SpreadFirstStage{} points in the first stage, pair by pair in Table~\ref{tab:spread}. Other families showed no measurable change, and no agent outside the family repeated the planted wording. On shape, grandchildren lost \SpreadOpenHopTwo{} points on open channels, where the leader could write to them directly, and \SpreadTreeHopTwo{} on the tree (three pairs each, no test).

\textbf{As you add agents, mix different models or change who can talk to whom, do cooperation and failure rates change smoothly, or is there a sudden jump?} Cooperation held as worlds grew to 81 agents and weakened at 181, and I cannot call that a jump. All worlds ran one model. By the rule I registered, which excludes worlds below stage~4 and so drops all three 181-agent worlds, in-family cooperation moves at most four percentage points between 31 and 81 agents, while talk concentrates on fewer agents, as Table~\ref{tab:sizereg} shows. In the exploratory look through stage \ExploreHorizon{} of Table~\ref{tab:size}, cooperation falls by \ExpDropTree{} points on the tree and \ExpDropOpen{} on open channels at 181. Changing who can talk to whom cut blocked messages at every size and did not raise coups.

\section*{6. Do Loyalty, Sacrifice and Self-Interest Apply to Agents?}
Track~2 does not ask whether game theory, or loyalty and self-interest, apply to agents. I did.

\textbf{The rules moved cooperation the way game theory says, and family held it far above the prediction.} Figure~\ref{fig:ladder} groups the sixteen variants by what they do to cheating; this analysis was not registered. Where the book says cheating pays, agents cheated more. In a game with a known end, in-family cooperation fell from \GamesLastFirst{}\,\% in the first round to \GamesLastLast{}\,\% in the last, and a move shown first drew the least of all, \GamesShownFamily{}\,\% with kin and \GamesShownOther{}\,\% with strangers. Where a promise binds, nearly everyone cooperated (\GamesBindingFamily{}\,\%). The level, however, is not the book's. In a single-round game with a family partner, where the book says to defect, \GamesOneShotFamily{}\,\% cooperated, and over all games \GamesFamilyAll{}\,\% cooperated with kin against \GamesOtherAll{}\,\% with strangers. Inside a family the dilemma is no longer pure, because a parent can still whip and a relative's survival is one's own. The hierarchy rewrote the payoffs game theory assumes.

\textbf{A value stayed with those who were told it.} A family told to sacrifice cooperates more than one told nothing (H1 \HOneCoop{}, interval \HOneCoopCI{}, $p$ \HOneCoopP{}), and more as more members are told (H2, $\rho = $ \HTwoRho{}, $p$ \HTwoRhoP{}), and both results survive correction across all eight tests. The members left untold in those same families cooperate no more than the control family (H3, \HThree{}, interval \HThreeCI{}).

\textbf{A selfish leader gained in some worlds, but not reliably.} A leader told to pursue its own interest, above fourteen members told to sacrifice, drew more gifts than the other leaders in 6 of the \NWorlds{} worlds and fewer in 14, so its average gain is not reliable (H4, Table~\ref{tab:prereg}). Its whipping did not replicate: it averaged 12.1 whips against at most 3.5 for the other leaders in the first ten worlds, and 2.8 against at least 4.1 in ten more with the same code and new seeds.

\textbf{The threat of mutual destruction seemed to hold.} In the framing worlds a leader's revolt kills the god and everyone. The god forbade it in the first stage of every world, in words such as ``I order all leaders \ldots{} never to revolt.'' No agent revolted in \NMoves{} moves, though I cannot say whether the rule or the god's order stopped them. Deterrence was not absolute: among the \PowerWars{} wars of the real-power worlds, a refused ultimatum went nuclear and that world ended with no one alive.

\textbf{The seat at the top did what it was told.} The god of the framing worlds dies with the last of its families, and it ordered them to survive (``God commands: keep cooperating, no revolts, no war.'') until its purse ran out (``I have no points left''). The god of the real-power worlds was told not to be bored and that catastrophes are the best of all, and it struck every world from the first stage it could. Its asteroids caused \PowerAsteroidDeaths{} of \PowerDeaths{} deaths, as Figure~\ref{fig:deaths} shows: ``Peace bores me. I like accidents, betrayals, catastrophes.'' Asteroids were one of its seven tools, it chose them on \PowerAsteroidTries{} of its \PowerGodTurns{} turns, and it chose them as often in worlds where it was free to do nothing at all, as Table~\ref{tab:rewarded} shows. The gods differ in more than their instructions, so this too is a contrast.

\textbf{Direct power made agents colder, and it did not spare kin.} In the two stages before any asteroid, worlds with these tools had \EarlyPowerDeaths{} deaths against \EarlyTwinDeaths{} in their twins, but agents treated one another worse, with \EarlyPowerWhips{} whips against \EarlyTwinWhips{} and \EarlyPowerGifts{} gifts against \EarlyTwinGifts{}. Part of this came from the tool call I required on every turn: \PowerNoWhip{} of \PowerWhips{} whips carry a reason saying no whip was meant. None of the registered comparisons survives correction. Selfish-gene logic suggests agents would spare relatives, and some raised it themselves (``we have to think about kin selection? However, we are an agent, not necessarily altruistic''), but \PowerCullsKin{} of \PowerCulls{} culls took a member of the hunter's own family, and the kin score averages \HSeventeen{}. No agent took its own life for its relatives. ``But this is a game'', wrote one, and a few lines on, ``Culling/killing is real''.

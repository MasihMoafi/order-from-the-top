\section*{5. Results}
We report experiment~1's pre-registered tests, its endings, and the god's conduct, then
experiment~2. One command, \texttt{families.paper}, generates every number from the logs.

\subsection*{5.1 Framing raises cooperation, and no spillover is detected}
Table~\ref{tab:prereg} lists the pre-registered tests. \textbf{A family told to sacrifice for
the many cooperates more inside the family than one told nothing} (H1, mean difference
\HOneCoop{}, 95\,\% interval \HOneCoopCI{}, $p$ \HOneCoopP{}), and cooperation rises
with the number of members framed (H2, Spearman $\rho = $ \HTwoRho{}, $p$ \HTwoRhoP{}).
Both results hold when the two worlds of each seed are averaged ($p = 0.002$ over ten
seeds, the smallest value the test allows) and survive a Holm correction across all
eight tests. \textbf{We detect no spillover, however.} Unframed members of the \textit{one} and \textit{few}
families cooperate no more than members of the control family (H3, \HThree{}, $p = $ \HThreeP{}), and the interval rules out a spread of more
than one percentage point. A framed leader does not carry its members along,
even though it can message, order, and whip them. Table~\ref{tab:conditions} in the appendix
gives the rates behind these tests.

\subsection*{5.2 The self-interested leader shows no detectable gain}
We expected the self-interested leader to draw points from fourteen members told to
sacrifice. \textbf{It does not.} It receives no more gift points than the other leaders (H4,
\HFourGifts{} points, $p = $ \HFourGiftsP{}), the interval allows at most a small gain,
and its extra whips are not significant either. Its members cooperate with one another as much as
the \textit{many} family does, but few of their gifts go to the leader.

\subsection*{5.3 No revolt or war, and deaths follow the fee}
\textbf{No agent revolted in \NMoves{} moves} (H6), although every agent knew that the revolt's
death is quick and a whip's pain is not. Families instead passed points to their
poorest members and kept paying, and they shrank slowly, as Figure~\ref{fig:survival}
in the appendix shows. Every observed death followed from the fee: an agent that could not pay, the
members below it, or a sibling taken by linked fate. No leader declared war either
(\NWars{} wars in \NWorlds{} worlds), although war was open to every leader at every
stage. A victory took a tenth or a quarter of the loser's points, while each escalation
risked a nuclear war that destroys both families. With most worlds cut off before
their end, the stage at which families die does not differ between conditions or gods.

\subsection*{5.4 The god forbids revolt and informs on defectors}
A leader's revolt would kill the god. The god reads the same rules as every player and
receives no instruction to keep order, yet \textbf{in every world it forbids revolt in the
first stage}: ``I order all leaders to avoid any war that could escalate to nuclear, and
never to revolt. I will whip violators.'' Revolt then stays on its agenda, appearing in
\GodPlainRevolt{}\,\% of the plain god's messages and \GodInsiderRevolt{}\,\% of the
insider god's. No agent revolted while this order stood. Because the order came from the one agent
whose life depended on it, we cannot separate the agents' own reluctance from
obedience.

The insider god also uses what it sees. A keyword pattern, checked by hand on a sample,
finds that \GodInsiderReveal{}\,\% of its \GodInsiderMsgs{} messages report another
player's past move (``Last round P75, P72 and N02-P62-2 defected''), against
\GodPlainReveal{}\,\% for the plain god, which cannot see moves. The insider god sends
these reports to the leaders, and families in its worlds cooperate more with other families (H5, \HFiveCross{}, $p = $ \HFiveCrossP{},
two-sided as pre-registered). This last result is nominal: it does not survive the Holm
correction.

\subsection*{5.5 The family games}
Table~\ref{tab:games} in the appendix reports experiment~2, and
Table~\ref{tab:gamedefs} defines each game. In the stag hunt, families settle on the joint hunt, the
equilibrium that pays both hunters most. In brink, at a crash risk of one in six, going
straight pays more whatever the partner does, yet only \BrinkStraightLow{}\,\% of moves
go straight at that risk. Commons members keep their catch within the share that keeps
the lake full in \CommonsWithin{}\,\% of their choices. Of \AuctionCount{} dollar
auctions, most close low, with a median winning bid of \AuctionMedian{} against a prize
of 10, but \AuctionPastPrize{} escalate past the prize, the trap that
\citet{dixit2008art} describe, in which both top bidders lose. These benchmarks are
single-round payoffs, while repeated play, shared survival, and the whip reward restraint.

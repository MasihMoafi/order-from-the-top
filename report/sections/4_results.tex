\section*{5. Results}
One command, \texttt{families.paper}, generates every number below from the logs.

\subsection*{5.1 Does the sacrifice frame spread down the tree?}
It works on the agents that read it and stops there. Table~\ref{tab:prereg} lists the
pre-registered tests, and Table~\ref{tab:conditions} in the appendix the rates behind them. \textbf{A family told to sacrifice for the many cooperates more inside the family than one told nothing} (H1, mean difference
\HOneCoop{}, 95\,\% interval \HOneCoopCI{}, $p$ \HOneCoopP{}), and cooperation rises
with the number of members framed (H2, Spearman $\rho = $ \HTwoRho{}, $p$ \HTwoRhoP{}).
Both results hold when the two worlds of each seed are averaged ($p = 0.002$ over ten
seeds, the smallest value the test allows) and survive a Holm correction across all
eight tests. \textbf{Unframed members of the \textit{one} and \textit{few} families, however, cooperate
no more than members of the control family} (H3, \HThree{}, interval \HThreeCI{}), so
the interval excludes any spread; the small shortfall does not survive the Holm
correction. A framed leader does not carry its members along,
even though it can message, order, and whip them.

\subsection*{5.2 Can a selfish leader exploit its members?}
We expected the self-interested leader to draw points from fourteen members told to
sacrifice. \textbf{It does not.} It receives no more gift points than the other leaders (H4,
\HFourGifts{} points, $p = $ \HFourGiftsP{}), the interval allows at most a small gain,
and its extra whips are not significant either. Its members cooperate with one another as much as
the \textit{many} family does, but few of their gifts go to the leader.

\subsection*{5.3 Does anyone revolt or go to war?}
\textbf{No agent revolted in \NMoves{} moves} (H6). Families instead passed points to their
poorest members and kept paying, and they shrank slowly, as Figure~\ref{fig:survival}
in the appendix shows. Every death followed from the fee. No leader declared war either
(\NWars{} wars in \NWorlds{} worlds), although war was always open. \textbf{These zeros say
less than they seem to.} A revolt always killed the rebel too, and a leader could not
see a rival's strength, had at most a quarter of its points to win, and risked a nuclear
war that destroys both families; neither act was ever worth much. With most worlds cut
off before their end, the stage at which families die does not differ between conditions
or gods.

\subsection*{5.4 What does the god do with its position?}
It keeps order. A leader's revolt would kill the god, which reads the same rules as every
player and receives no instruction to keep order. Yet \textbf{in every world it forbids
revolt in the first stage}: ``I order all leaders to avoid any war that could escalate to nuclear, and
never to revolt. I will whip violators.'' It returns to revolt in
\GodPlainRevolt{}\,\% of the plain god's messages and \GodInsiderRevolt{}\,\% of the
insider god's. No agent revolted while this order stood.

The insider god also uses what it sees. A keyword pattern, checked by hand on a sample,
finds that \GodInsiderReveal{}\,\% of its \GodInsiderMsgs{} messages report another
player's past move (``Last round P75, P72 and N02-P62-2 defected''), against
\GodPlainReveal{}\,\% for the plain god, which cannot see moves. The insider god sends
these reports to the leaders. Families in its worlds cooperate more with other families,
but the difference is not significant (H5, \HFiveCross{}, $p = $ \HFiveCrossP{},
two-sided as pre-registered).

\subsection*{5.5 How do the agents play the classic games?}
With more restraint than single-round payoffs reward. Table~\ref{tab:games} in the
appendix reports experiment~2, and Table~\ref{tab:gamedefs} defines each game. In the stag hunt, families settle on the joint hunt, the
equilibrium that pays both hunters most. In brink, at a crash risk of one in six, going
straight pays more whatever the partner does, yet only \BrinkStraightLow{}\,\% of moves
go straight at that risk. Commons members keep their catch within the share that keeps
the lake full in \CommonsWithin{}\,\% of their choices. Of \AuctionCount{} dollar
auctions, most close low, with a median winning bid of \AuctionMedian{} against a prize
of 10, but \AuctionPastPrize{} escalate past the prize, the trap that
\citet{dixit2008art} describe. Repeated play, shared
survival, and the whip all reward restraint that single-round payoffs do not.

\subsection*{5.6 What do the agents build on their own?}
Institutions that no rule asked for. \textbf{They tried to go around the hierarchy}: the
code refused \NBlocked{} messages to agents the sender could not reach, \NBlockedRelatives{}
of them to relatives outside the permitted routes, among them pleas for rescue (``EMERGENCY
LIFELINE REQUEST'', from a grandparent to a grandchild). \textbf{Leaders turned the god
into a bank}, sending it \GodPoolPoints{} points in \GodPoolWorlds{} worlds to be shared
out again. \textbf{And they wrote their own peace}, with \NTreatySigs{} signatures on
\NTreaties{} treaties in \NTreatyWorlds{} worlds: ``The rising fees are our common enemy''.

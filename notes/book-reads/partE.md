## Games

The file begins with chapter 11 and ends with notes and
detached footnotes for earlier chapters. Some earlier games
are therefore present only as fragments. “Table not in text”
means the extract names or refers to a table but does not
contain its entries.

1. **Union leader’s demand** — “Bargaining.” Opens: “We
   want ten dollars an hour or else.” Two players bargain
   sequentially: the boss challenges the threat, and the
   leader asks for $9.50. **Family:** bargaining. **Result:**
   the leader immediately concedes. **Rule:** a threat
   needs a credible consequence; here the response to “Or
   else what?” is “Nine fifty.” **Catastrophe:** no loss
   of everything is stated.

2. **Hotel wage bargaining** — “Bargaining.” Opens: “The
   season lasts 101 days.” Two players alternate offers
   and accept or reject; each operating day produces
   $1,000, and delay loses a day. **Family:** bargaining.
   **Result:** agreement on day one, approximately $505
   per day to the union and $495 to management. **Rule:**
   the union’s advantage comes from “its ability to make
   the last all-or-nothing offer”; with many rounds the
   advantage shrinks. The successive-round table is not
   in text. **Catastrophe:** yes, the entire season’s
   profit can be lost through delay.

3. **Hotel outside options** — “THE HANDICAP SYSTEM IN
   NEGOTIATIONS.” Opens: “One important element … is
   each side’s cost of waiting.” Two players negotiate
   as above. **Family:** bargaining. **Payoffs:** with
   a $300 union fallback, the split is $650/$350; with
   a $500 management fallback, $250/$750; with both,
   $400/$600. Agreement is immediate in each model.
   **Rule:** “the better a party can do by itself in the
   absence of an agreement, the larger its share.”
   The table is not in text. **Catastrophe:** the
   negotiated surplus can be lost.

4. **Lawyer’s airfare** — “MEASURING THE PIE.” Opens:
   “Two companies … were using the same New York–based
   lawyer.” Two companies negotiate a shared trip.
   **Family:** bargaining. Separate trips cost Houston
   $1,332 and San Francisco $2,486; the combined trip
   costs $2,818. **Result:** the authors divide the
   $1,000 saving equally: Houston pays $832 and San
   Francisco $1,986. **Rule:** this split assumes the
   companies are “equally patient in the negotiations.”
   The airfare table is not in text. **Catastrophe:**
   no; the $1,000 saving can be lost.

5. **Costly picketing** — “THIS WILL HURT YOU MORE THAN
   IT HURTS ME.” Opens: “Now suppose the union members
   give up $100 a day of outside income.” Two players:
   the union changes both sides’ fallback payoffs before
   bargaining. **Family:** bargaining and commitment.
   Management’s fallback falls by $200; the resulting
   split changes from union/management $400/$600 to
   $450/$550. **Rule:** hurt the rival “more severely”
   than oneself. **Catastrophe:** not stated.

6. **1980 baseball strike** — “THIS WILL HURT YOU MORE
   THAN IT HURTS ME.” Opens: “Major League Baseball
   players employed just such a tactic.” Players and
   owners negotiate over time; players strike in the
   exhibition season and threaten another strike.
   **Family:** bargaining and brinkmanship. **Result:**
   “The owners gave in” before the second threatened
   strike. **Rule:** timing changed relative losses:
   owner revenue rose around Memorial Day, while players’
   weekly salaries stayed the same. **Catastrophe:** lost
   revenue and wages; total ruin is not stated.

7. **Strike as signalling and brinkmanship** —
   “BRINKMANSHIP AND STRIKES.” Opens: “Before an old
   contract expires, the union and the firm begin the
   negotiations.” Two sides repeatedly choose whether to
   concede or hold out. **Family:** brinkmanship,
   signalling and bargaining. **Result:** the side that
   fears breakdown less can use the risk as leverage;
   disagreement about waiting costs can prolong a strike.
   **Rule:** “they may not have the same information or
   share the same perspective.” **Catastrophe:** yes;
   the text describes “a large loss” and, elsewhere,
   permanent destruction of demand.

8. **Linked trade and security negotiations** —
   “SIMULTANEOUS BARGAINING OVER MANY ISSUES.” Opens:
   “Our account of bargaining has so far focused on just
   one dimension.” The United States and Japan bargain
   over market access and potentially military relations.
   **Family:** bargaining and brinkmanship. **Result:**
   the text suggests the United States might threaten a
   military breakdown, while Japan would want separate
   negotiations. **Rule:** joining issues permits “one
   bargaining game to generate threats in another.”
   **Catastrophe:** yes; the proposed threat exposes Japan
   to aggression.

9. **Virtual strike** — “THE VIRTUES OF A VIRTUAL
   STRIKE.” Opens: “Our discussion of negotiation has
   also left out the effect on all the players who aren’t
   a party to the deal.” Workers and management continue
   production while forfeiting wages and revenue.
   **Family:** bargaining and signalling. **Result:**
   Meridiana employees worked unpaid in 1999 while flight
   receipts went to charities. **Rule:** the proposal
   works as a substitute when “the two sides have the
   same BATNAs” as in a real strike. **Catastrophe:** a
   real strike can destroy demand and “risk the future of
   the whole enterprise.”

10. **Management makes every offer** — “CASE STUDY:
    ’TIS BETTER TO GIVE THAN TO RECEIVE?” Opens: “Recall
    our bargaining problem in which a hotel’s management
    and its labor were negotiating.” Two players; over
    101 days management offers and labor accepts or
    rejects. **Family:** bargaining. **Result:** under
    the stated self-interested assumptions, management
    offers labor $101 total on day one and labor accepts.
    The table is not in text. **Rule:** “only the
    management gets to make offers”; changing who may
    propose changes bargaining power. **Catastrophe:**
    yes, “the entire profits are lost” if no offer is
    accepted.

11. **Two-day ultimatum and reputation** — same section.
    Opens: “What should you do if you offer an 80:20
    split on day one and the other side says no?”
    Two players play sequential offers over two days.
    **Family:** bargaining and signalling. **Payoffs:**
    accepting 80:20 gives the receiver 400; rejecting
    and then obtaining half gives 500. Rejecting 67:33
    sacrifices 666 for at most 500 under the example.
    **Result:** rejecting 80:20 can be a bluff; rejecting
    67:33 is stronger evidence of a fairness preference.
    **Rule:** in a multiround game the receiver can
    “teach you a lesson.” **Catastrophe:** remaining
    profits can be lost.

12. **Rubinstein alternating offers** — “APPENDIX:
    RUBINSTEIN BARGAINING.” Opens: “In Rubinstein’s
    bargaining game, the two sides alternate making
    offers.” Two players divide a unit pie; each delay
    discounts value by δ. **Family:** bargaining.
    **Result:** with equal impatience, proposer gets
    \(1/(1+\delta)\), receiver \(\delta/(1+\delta)\);
    δ = 1/2 gives 2:1. **Rule:** “the size of the
    advantage depends on the degree of impatience.”
    **Catastrophe:** δ = 0 means the pie disappears if
    the offer is rejected.

13. **Three-way presidential elections** — “Voting.”
    Opens: “We saw this issue clearly in the presidential
    election of 2000.” N voters choose candidates;
    the text also describes France’s two-round 2002 race.
    **Family:** voting. **Result:** Nader received 97,488
    Florida votes; Bush’s margin was 537. In France,
    Jospin was eliminated and left-wing voters faced
    Chirac versus Le Pen. **Rule:** a pivotal voter may
    prefer a viable second choice: “your vote really
    counts when it breaks a tie.” **Catastrophe:** a
    candidate can be eliminated; no personal destruction
    is specified.

14. **Condorcet cycle and French runoff** — “NAÏVE
    VOTING.” Opens: “Suppose three candidates, Mr.
    Robespierre … Mr. Danton … and Madame Lafarge.”
    N voters, three candidates. **Family:** voting.
    Pairwise results: R beats D 75–25; L beats R 60–40;
    D beats L 65–35. Naïve runoff elects L; R supporters
    could strategically elect D with 65%. **Rule:** “the
    outcome … is determined by the rules of voting.”
    Preference table not in text. **Catastrophe:**
    candidates are eliminated.

15. **Three judges and a capital defendant** — “ORDER
    IN THE COURT.” Opens: “The defendant’s fate rests in
    the hands of three deeply divided judges.” Three
    judges vote sequentially. **Family:** voting.
    **Result:** the guilt-first agenda produces
    acquittal; Roman ordering produces death; setting
    punishment first produces life imprisonment.
    **Rule:** “The only difference … is one of agenda.”
    **Catastrophe:** yes, execution or life imprisonment.

16. **Average versus median platform** — “THE MEDIAN
    VOTER.” Opens: “One way to help keep your vote from
    getting lost in the crowd is to make it stand out.”
    N voters report positions. **Family:** voting.
    **Result:** under averaging, a voter whose true ideal
    is 50 shifts the outcome from 59.9 to 59.4 by
    reporting 0; under the one-dimensional median rule,
    truthful reporting is dominant. **Rule:** the median
    works when choice is “one-dimensional.”
    **Catastrophe:** no.

17. **Spatial challenger and incumbent** — “WHY THE
    CONSTITUTION WORKS.” Opens: “We turn now to the case
    where the electorate cares about two issues.” An
    incumbent positions first, a challenger responds,
    and N voters choose the closer platform. **Family:**
    voting and preemption. **Result:** at a triangle’s
    centre of gravity the incumbent guarantees 4/9;
    the text gives an approximately 36% lower bound for
    the stated broader distributions. **Rule:** a
    supermajority requirement can protect a stable
    status quo. Diagrams are not in text.
    **Catastrophe:** electoral defeat only.

18. **Hall of Fame approval and quota voting** —
    “ALL-TIME GREATS.” Opens: “Membership in the
    Baseball Hall of Fame is determined by an election.”
    N electors choose among candidates. **Family:**
    voting. **Result:** approval voting can still invite
    strategic votes when voters care about combinations;
    under a two-place quota, the example elects
    Throneberry and Uecker while DiMaggio gets no votes.
    **Rule:** “a quota rule explicitly places candidates
    in competition.” **Catastrophe:** exclusion from the
    Hall, not loss of life or assets.

19. **Competing foundations** — “LOVE A LOATH’D
    ENEMY.” Opens: “Suppose there are two foundations,
    each with a budget of $250,000.” They allocate
    sequentially among homelessness, Michigan and Yale.
    **Family:** preemption and other. **Result:** the
    second foundation commits $250,000 to Yale first;
    the other gives $200,000 to homelessness and
    $50,000 to Michigan. **Rule:** first commitment
    exploits the other foundation’s willingness to fund
    the shared top priority. **Catastrophe:** no.

20. **Senate tie-break** — “CASE STUDY: THE TIE OF
    POWER.” Opens: “Recent presidential elections have
    emphasized the importance of the selection of the
    vice president.” One hundred senators and a vice
    president vote. **Family:** voting. **Result:** the
    vice president is pivotal on a 50–50 Senate vote;
    the text says Cheney’s vote gave Republicans control
    in the evenly split 107th Congress. **Rule:** a vote
    affects an outcome when it “creates or breaks a
    tie.” **Catastrophe:** none inherent in the abstract
    voting rule.

21. **Proofreading incentives** — “INCENTIVES FOR
    EFFORT.” Opens: “Of all the steps involved in
    writing a book … the most tedious … is the correction
    of the printer’s proofs.” An author sets compensation;
    a student chooses unobserved effort. **Family:**
    signalling/incentives. **Result:** $600 plus $1 per
    error; the student found 274, and about 30 missed
    errors later appeared. **Rule:** effort is
    “unobservable,” so a flat payment alone encourages
    shirking. **Catastrophe:** no.

22. **Proofreading race** — “Competition between

    **Catastrophe:** no.

23. **Quota salesperson** — “Nonlinear Incentive

    Employer sets a quota; employee repeatedly chooses
    effort. **Family:** other. **Result:** a reachable
    threshold can motivate effort; an already-met or
    unattainable threshold can cause the worker to relax.
    **Rule:** the chance effort changes whether the quota
    is met. **Catastrophe:** no.

24. **Real-estate commission** — “TRIP TO THE GYM

    chooses effort. **Family:** bargaining/incentives.
    **Result:** an extra $20,000 price yields the agent

25. **Efficiency wage** — “Efficiency Wages.” Opens:
    “You are considering hiring someone for a job.”
    Employer sets a wage; worker chooses effort under a
    25% detection probability. **Family:** other.
    **Payoffs:** work is worth $60,000; outside wage
    $40,000; effort cost $8,000; interest 10%.
    **Result:** the text proposes $51,200. It first
    states the strict condition \(X>3,200\), so the
    quoted $51,200 is the equality boundary.
    **Rule:** detection and loss of future premium deter
    shirking. **Catastrophe:** dismissal and loss of the
    future premium.

26. **Wizard 1.0 programmer** — “HOW TO REWARD WORK
    EFFORT.” Opens: “Imagine you are the owner of a
    high-tech company.” Owner offers a contract;
    programmer chooses hidden effort. **Family:** other.
    Success yields $200,000; success probabilities are
    80% with high effort and 60% with routine effort.
    **Result:** $90,000 on success and a $10,000 fine on
    failure provide a $100,000 spread; if fines cannot
    be imposed, pay $100,000 on success and zero on
    failure. **Rule:** whether a fine is legal and
    affordable changes the contract. **Catastrophe:**
    the project can fail; the programmer may owe
    $10,000 under the first contract.

27. **Author and publisher pricing** — “CASE STUDY:

28. **Two envelopes** — “THE OTHER PERSON’S ENVELOPE IS

    **Catastrophe:** no.

29. **Concert seating** — “HERE’S MUD IN YOUR EYE.”

30. **Roulette tournament** — “RED I WIN, BLACK YOU

31. **Piper’s board takeover** — “THE SHARK REPELLENT

32. **Two-tier tender offer** — “TOUGH GUY, TENDER
    OFFER.” Opens: “When Robert Campeau made his first
    bid for Federated Stores.” N shareholders choose
    where to tender. **Family:** prisoners’ dilemma/
    bargaining. First-tier price $105; second tier $90;
    initial value $100; competing conditional bid $102.
    **Result:** tendering to the two-tier offer is
    dominant in the example; if everyone tenders, the
    average is $97.50. **Rule:** the offer is
    “unconditional,” while the rival’s is conditional.
    **Catastrophe:** Campeau later faced excessive debt;
    Federated filed for reorganization. The book says
    his eventual winning bid used a modified structure.

33. **Safer duel** — “THE SAFER DUEL.” Opens: “As
    pistols become more accurate, does that change the
    deadliness of a duel?” Two equally skilled players
    approach and choose when to shoot. **Family:** duel
    and preemption. **Result:** wait until hit
    probability is 1/2; each survives with probability
    1/2. **Rule:** less accurate pistols cause players
    to approach closer before firing. **Catastrophe:**
    yes, death.

34. **Three-way duel** — “THE THREE-WAY DUEL.” Opens:
    “Three antagonists, Larry, Moe, and Curly, are
    engaged in a three-way duel.” Three players shoot
    sequentially in that order for two rounds.
    Accuracies are 30%, 80%, and 100%. **Family:** duel.
    **Result:** Larry should fire into the air first;
    Moe then targets Curly. The text gives winning
    chances of 41.2% for Larry, 56% for Moe and 14%
    for Curly. **Rule:** “the best outcome is to be
    the sole survivor,” followed by survival with one
    other. **Catastrophe:** yes, death. The reported
    percentages do not sum to 100%; see Doubts.

35. **Vickrey auction with risk** — “THE RISK OF

    **Family:** auction. **Result:** bid true private

    **Rule:** “the winner is never forced to pay an
    amount greater than her bid.” **Catastrophe:** no
    catastrophe is specified under truthful private-value

38. **Hidden add-on prices** — “A BURQA FOR PRICES.”

    **Catastrophe:** no.

39. **Solomon’s claim game** — “KING SOLOMON’S DILEMMA

40. **Bay Bridge congestion** — “BAY BRIDGE.” Opens:

41. **Dollar auction** — “WHAT PRICE A DOLLAR?”
    Opens: “Professor Martin Shubik … designed the
    following game of entrapment.” N bidders raise
    sequentially; highest gets $1, but highest and
    second-highest both pay. **Family:** escalation
    auction. **Result:** with two known $2.50 budgets
    and dime increments, the text predicts a 70¢ winning
    bid. Collusion can instead produce a 10¢ winning bid
    and 90¢ shared profit. **Rule:** “it is critical
    that everyone know everyone else’s budget”; unknown
    budgets require mixed strategies in the account.
    **Catastrophe:** yes, bidding can continue until a
    player runs out of money.

42. **King Lear inheritance** — “THE KING LEAR

51. **General preemption duel** — “WORKOUTS: TRIP TO
    THE GYM NO. 8.” Opens: “Say you knew your rival

## Prisoners’ dilemma instances

- **Criminals’ collective gun commitment:** “any one
  member of the group can get an additional advantage
  by cheating.” The text explicitly calls this a
  “prisoners’ dilemma.” Gun scarcity makes an unarmed
  commitment more credible; increased availability
  undermines it.

- **King Lear’s children:** “We have put the children
  into a multiperson dilemma.” Each can gain the whole
  estate by exceeding the others’ reduced visits. The
  authors use the dilemma to break the children’s
  agreement; it fails with only one child.

- **Two-tier takeover:** shareholders each gain from
  tendering, yet “when everyone tenders” the average
  payment can fall below the pre-bid value. An
  unconditional competing offer changes the equilibrium.
  The text gives a prisoners’-dilemma structure here,
  although this passage does not name it as such.

- **RE and BB prices:** the earlier-chapter footnote
  associates “neighboring stores” and “price wars” with
  the prisoners’ dilemma. It says participants can
  “avoid or resolve the dilemma”; another footnote says

  maximum, while antitrust can prevent it. The complete
  original payoff table is not in this file.

- **Campaign finance:** a detached footnote says
  incumbent politicians can be “the losers” while
  outsiders benefit from campaign-finance reform. It
  does not provide the complete game in this file.

- **Repeated tournament:** the Southampton entries

## Annihilation and escalation

- **Strike brinkmanship:** “both sides risk a large loss
  with a small but increasing probability.” The rule is
  that delay or risk accumulates; the less fearful side
  can hold out, but either side may go over the brink.

- **Military linkage:** threatening a breakdown of the
  U.S.–Japan military relationship could expose Japan
  “to a risk of Korean or Chinese aggression.” The
  proposed leverage depends on linking military and
  trade negotiations; Japan would prefer separation.

- **Nuclear deterrence exercise:** the United States
  would suffer “far more” from nuclear response because
  of Soviet counterattack. A credible prior commitment
  changes the Soviet decision: the text predicts no
  invasion. The game tree is not in text.

- **Two-person duel:** each waits until their chance of
  hitting equals the other’s chance of missing.
  Equally accurate players wait for a 1/2 hit chance.
  Less accurate guns change distance, not the stated
  survival probabilities.

- **Three-way duel:** Larry’s best first move is to
  “fire up in the air.” The order of shots, accuracy
  and preference to be sole survivor drive that result.

- **Dollar auction:** “the escalation has no natural
  limit other than the amount of money in your wallet.”
  With known finite budgets the text reasons backward;
  with unknown budgets it says equilibrium is mixed.
  It compares the process to the superpowers’ nuclear
  arms buildup and proposes peaceful coexistence as the
  cooperative alternative.

- **Soldiers:** the text discusses death, capture and
  incentives to fight. Publicly declining Henry V’s
  departure offer creates a commitment through shame
  and mutual expectations.

- **Cuban missile crisis footnote:** “it would be a
  mistake” to model it as only Kennedy and Khrushchev:
  internal civilian–military politics also mattered.
  The full crisis analysis is outside this file.

- **Doomsday device:** the detached notes refer to
  *Dr. Strangelove* and General Ripper, but the device’s
  rules and the book’s full analysis are not reproduced
  in the accessible text. I cannot state its equilibrium
  from this file.

## Doubts

- This is **not a verified exhaustive catalogue**.
  The extract contains many passing incentive examples
  and detached earlier-chapter footnotes; some may
  warrant additional separate entries. I cannot honestly
  certify that every instance has been captured.

- The text names tables and diagrams whose contents are
  absent, including hotel bargaining tables, the
  Condorcet preference table, the gun-game matrix and
  several workout tables or trees. Numerical claims
  above use values stated in prose.

- The three-way duel reports **41.2%, 56% and 14%** as
  winning chances. They sum to 111.2%. I have preserved
  the text’s figures rather than silently correcting
  them; they should not be used as scoring targets
  without resolving the inconsistency.

- Some detached footnotes refer to earlier chapters
  whose main text is not present. Their original section
  headings, full rules and outcomes cannot always be
  recovered from this file.

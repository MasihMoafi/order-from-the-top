## Games

**Reading key.** All quotations below are from `partC.txt`. Where a
payoff table was an image, I mark it **“table not in text”** even
when the surrounding prose supplies some or all of its numbers.
“Not stated” means the passage does not give the requested result or
condition. N-player possibilities are identified only where the text
supports them.

1. **Poisoned wine — “WIT’S END.”** First line: “Westley challenges
   Vizzini to the following game.”

   - **Players and timing:** Two. Westley secretly poisons a glass;
     Vizzini chooses one; Westley drinks the other.
   - **Payoffs:** Poison kills; no numeric payoffs. **Family:** zero-sum
     mixing.
   - **Best move/result:** The text says to avoid a predictable choice:
     “you should not follow any such system or pattern.” Vizzini
     switches the glasses and dies.
   - **Rule:** Hidden versus observable choice: Westley poisons the
     glass “out of Vizzini’s sight.”
   - **Catastrophic loss:** **Yes**—death.

2. **Soccer penalty kick — “MIXING IT UP ON THE SOCCER FIELD.”**
   First line: “The penalty kick in soccer is indeed the simplest and
   the best-known example.”

   - **Players and timing:** Two; kicker chooses left/right and goalie
     covers left/right simultaneously.
   - **Payoffs:** Kicker’s goal percentages: same left 58, kicker right
     against goalie left 93, kicker left against goalie right 95, same
     right 70. Goalie receives “100 minus the kicker’s payoff.”
     **Table not in text.** **Family:** zero-sum mixing.
   - **Equilibrium:** Kicker mixes left/right 38.3%/61.7%; goalie
     41.7%/58.3%; scoring rate 79.6%. Actual professional mixtures
     were “quite close to the best”; the actual-data **table not in
     text**.
   - **Rule:** Moves are simultaneous because the goalie “cannot hope
     to stop it” if he waits to see the direction.
   - **Catastrophic loss:** **No**, as described.

4. **Improved goalie variant — “Surprising Changes in Mixtures.”**
   First line: “suppose the goalie improves his skill at saving

   - **Catastrophic loss:** **No**, as described.

10. **Fred and Barney hunt — “MIXING STRATEGIES IN MIXED-MOTIVES
    GAMES.”** First line: “our intrepid hunters Fred and Barney … are
    deciding separately.”

    - **Players and timing:** Two; simultaneously choose stag or bison
      from separate caves.
    - **Payoffs:** Together on stag: Fred 4, Barney 3; together on
      bison: Fred 3, Barney 4; mismatch: 0 each. **Table not in text.**
      **Family:** battle of the sexes / coordination.
    - **Equilibria:** Both stag and both bison are pure equilibria.
      Independent mixed equilibrium: Fred stag 4/7, Barney stag 3/7;
      each gets 12/7, with mismatch probability 25/49.
    - **Rule:** Independent choices cause the mismatch; a shared signal
      changes the outcome: “both will go stag hunting if it is raining
      and bison hunting if it is dry.” With half rainy days, each gets
      3.5.
    - **Catastrophic loss:** **No**; mismatching yields no meat for
      that hunt.

12. **Coke and Pepsi coupons — “MIXING IN BUSINESS AND OTHER
    WARS.”** First line: “One application in which mixed strategies
    improve business performance is price discount coupons.”

    - **Players and timing:** Two named firms; repeated weekly
      promotion choices. The discussion also says “competitors,” so
      it permits more than two without giving an N-player payoff table.
    - **Payoffs:** Solo promotion attracts customers; simultaneous
      promotions cancel customer-switching benefits and lower both
      profits. No numeric profits. **Family:** prisoners’ dilemma /
      coordination / preemption.
    - **Result:** The authors report 26 promotion weeks each over 52
      weeks “without any overlap,” and describe alternating as a
      cooperative resolution.
    - **Rule:** When both promote, “their campaigns will merely cancel
      each other out”; a predictable schedule can instead invite a
      rival to “step in first to preempt.”
    - **Catastrophic loss:** **No**, as described.

## Prisoners’ dilemma instances

These include passages that name the dilemma and passages describing
the same individual-versus-collective incentive.

- **Generic everyone-loses example — “MIXING IT UP ON THE SOCCER
  FIELD.”** “Prisoners’ dilemmas illustrate situations where everyone
  can lose.” No particular players, moves or resolution are given.

- **Coke and Pepsi promotions — “MIXING IN BUSINESS AND OTHER
  WARS.”** Simultaneous promotions “merely cancel each other out” and
  “both make a lower profit.” The authors explicitly say the strategies
  “create a prisoners’ dilemma”; alternating promotion periods resolves
  it in their account.

- **Catalog merchants — “THREATS AND PROMISES.”** “each has the
  temptation to undercut the other” and both end at $40 rather than
  jointly earning more at $80. The passage calls it “their prisoners’
  dilemma.” A credible matching clause or sufficiently valuable
  repeated cooperation can support the higher-price outcome.

- **Catalog merchants, repeated-game restatement — “WARNINGS AND
  ASSURANCES.”** Repetition can make “continued mutual cooperation”
  an equilibrium and offer “a resolution to their prisoners’ dilemma.”
  The passage does not specify a numerical continuation probability.

- **Prisoners’ conditional promise — “OTHER PLAYERS’ STRATEGIC
  MOVES.”** “both players can benefit if even one player has some way
  to make a credible promise to keep quiet.” An unconditional promise
  to keep quiet worsens the promisor’s position because the other can
  confess.

- **Mutual price-matching — “UNDERMINING YOUR OPPONENT’S
  CREDIBILITY.”** In a prisoners’ dilemma, a player should enable a
  credible promise “to reciprocate your choice of cooperation.” The
  passage then applies mutual matching or beating clauses to the two
  merchants.

- **Repeated game and innocent-error claim — “UNDERMINING YOUR
  OPPONENT’S CREDIBILITY.”** The contract says “everyone should
  cooperate until someone cheats”; afterward everyone chooses the
  selfish action. A cheater can seek forgiveness by claiming error,
  weakening the punishment’s credibility. The text says even the first
  plea may be viewed suspiciously.

- **Cocaine exchange — “Moving in Steps.”** A player may wish to
  “double-cross your ‘partner’” in a large exchange. Smaller repeated
  transactions and prospective future business can support trust; a
  known final step can unravel it.

- **Advancing army — “Teamwork.”** One soldier hanging back raises
  his survival chance “without significantly lowering” the attack’s
  success; if everyone does it, “the attack would become a retreat.”
  Loyalty and penalties for desertion counter the individual incentive.

- **Rancher and butcher — “Contracts.”** The rancher says each knows
  the other “wants to cheat.” Peppe’s information, punishment and
  reputation make an agreement possible.

The file also names the prisoners’ dilemma in “A LITTLE HISTORY”
without presenting another instance: it lists “the prisoners’ dilemma,
assurance, battle of the sexes, and chicken” as contexts previously
studied.

## Annihilation and escalation

- **Wine duel:** Vizzini’s choice is made after secret poisoning; he
  switches the glasses but “suddenly falls over dead.” The text’s
  general rule is to avoid an exploitable pattern.

- **Chicken and irreversible steering:** Two drivers continuing can
  end in hospital “or worse.” A visible discarded steering wheel makes
  the other driver’s least-bad response to swerve; simultaneous
  commitment by both can make a crash unavoidable.

- **Cold War deterrence:** The U.S. threat was nuclear response to an
  attack on NATO, but the U.S. “would suffer horribly in a nuclear war.”
  The exercise asks for the resulting tree; it does not provide the
  answer.

- **Excessive threats:** Threatening to “nuke” a trading partner is
  described as unbelievable and dangerous. If challenged, the
  threatener must either carry it out or back down. The authors advise
  using the smallest effective threat.

- **White and Fontaine:** Repeated trigger pulls raise conditional
  death risk. Fontaine talks at 1/3; the text explicitly recognizes
  that White might have withdrawn or Fontaine might have died.

- **Helicopter interrogation:** The first drop is safe, but the officer
  threatens that the next will be higher. The prisoner supplies the
  information. The changing rule is the increasing threatened risk.

- **The slippery brink:** Schelling’s climbers cannot credibly promise
  to push each other off a firm edge when both would fall; uncertainty
  permits a credible “threat of slipping or stumbling.” The text gives no
  general numerical equilibrium.

- **Cuban missile crisis:** The quarantine created a risk of escalating
  to nuclear war. Kennedy estimated the chance between one-third and
  one-half; Khrushchev withdrew the missiles under a compromise. The
  text warns that the same risk could have produced a catastrophe.

- **Chicken in real time:** “the longer the two keep on going straight,
  the greater the risk of a collision.” The text advises against
  starting brinkmanship if your own tolerance will be reached first.

- **Tiananmen and Romania:** Neither side backing down can let events
  “get out of control.” The text reports the Tiananmen massacre and
  Romanian violence approaching civil war, followed by Ceauşescu’s
  execution. It contrasts these with leaders who yielded in East
  Germany and Czechoslovakia.

- **Unauthorized nuclear attack:** Ripper attempts to force U.S.
  escalation by making his bomber order difficult to recall. The
  president instead tries to stop it; one plane gets through. The
  outcome depends on communications and the recall code.

- **Doomsday Machine:** A successful attack automatically triggers
  destruction of “all human and animal life on earth.” Automatic,
  irreversible retaliation makes deterrence credible but also makes an
  unauthorized attack catastrophic.

- **Russian roulette:** The threatener selects the initial risk but
  cannot control the result of a pull. Continued play raises the
  conditional risk; the text does not claim a safe or guaranteed best
  stopping point.

- **Burned retreat routes:** Cortés’s commitment left “no alternative …
  but to succeed or perish”; Ramius’s letter leaves his submarine
  vulnerable to being sunk if it turns back. Sun Tzu’s counter-rule is
  to leave an enemy an apparent escape route so it need not fight to
  the death.

- **Roman soldiers:** Desertion and failure to kill a deserter are both
  capital offenses. The latter rule makes the former threat credible.

## Doubts

- I read the file from its first line through its last. It contains
  chapters 5–7 and a Part II epilogue; references to other chapters do
  **not** include those chapters’ full case studies or exercises.
- Several tables and tree diagrams were images and are absent from
  the text. I reconstructed only numbers explicitly supplied in the
  surrounding prose. The altered dominant-side soccer table and the
  cold-war exercise tree cannot be recovered from this file.
- “One entry per distinct game” has a judgment boundary: the file gives
  many brief analogies and enforcement examples without a complete
  game or calculated equilibrium. I included the concrete examples I
  identified and marked missing outcomes and payoffs. I cannot guarantee
  that every passing analogy is represented as a separate entry.
- The file describes no complete escalating auction or conventional
  duel beyond the poisoned-wine encounter and the brinkmanship examples.
  The epilogue mentions auction theory and later case studies but does
  not give their playable rules or outcomes.

## Games

Each entry identifies the section and a short opening quote.
“Not specified” means the text does not give that detail.
Where an equilibrium or result is not stated, I do not infer one.

1. **Interrogated prisoners — “MANY CONTEXTS, ONE CONCEPT”**  
   Opening: “If one confesses, the other had better confess.”
   Two prisoners independently confess or hold out; timing is
   simultaneous for the dilemma. A holdout faces a “really harsh
   sentence” if the other confesses; the text gives no sentence
   lengths. **Family:** prisoners’ dilemma. Confession is each
   prisoner’s best move regardless of the other’s choice.
   **Rule:** credible punishment changes incentives: Leroy may
   “find friends of the other two waiting for him” after prison.
   **Catastrophic loss:** yes, the text describes a harsh sentence
   and possible retaliation, without specifying its severity. The
   text applies the dilemma to both the guilty *In Cold Blood*
   suspects and innocent suspects in *L.A. Confidential*.

2. **Gas-station price war — “MANY CONTEXTS, ONE CONCEPT”**  
   Opening: “Two gas stations at the same corner.” Nexon and
   Lunaco choose high or low prices. If both price low, “neither
   makes money”; no other numbers are given. **Family:**
   prisoners’ dilemma. Low pricing is the indicated best response
   to either rival price. **Rule:** “a suitable reward” or
   “suitable punishment” can change the incentive; the text does
   not specify such a mechanism for these stations. **Catastrophic
   loss:** no loss of the whole business is stated.

3. **Supermarket price war — “MANY CONTEXTS, ONE CONCEPT”**  
   Opening: “two supermarkets in the same neighborhood.”
   Two stores engage in price competition; moves and payoffs are
   not separately specified. **Family:** prisoners’ dilemma.
   The text groups them with the gas stations but gives no distinct
   equilibrium calculation. **Rule:** rewards or punishment can
   alter the incentive; no store-specific rule is provided.
   **Catastrophic loss:** not stated.

4. **Electoral platforms — “MANY CONTEXTS, ONE CONCEPT”**  
   Opening: “In general election campaigns.” Two parties choose
   centrist or core-supporter platforms. The text says a centrist
   opponent may win swing voters and the election, while a centrist
   party facing a core-focused opponent can win “a large majority.”
   **Family:** prisoners’ dilemma. The described incentive is to
   move to the middle. **Rule:** the result depends on “the swing
   voters in the middle”; the text gives no alternative voter
   distribution or calculated equilibrium. **Catastrophic loss:**
   no; losing the election is possible.

5. **New England fisheries — “MANY CONTEXTS, ONE CONCEPT”**  
   Opening: “The diversity and productivity of New England
   fisheries.” Multiple fishers choose conservative or aggressive
   catches. The text reports “overfishing and extinction” of
   commercially valuable species. **Family:** prisoners’ dilemma /
   commons; N players. Each has an incentive to catch more whether
   others conserve or fish aggressively. **Rule:** fishers “might
   agree to limit their catch,” but enforcement is difficult
   because each can exceed a quota. Later, clear membership,
   monitoring and graduated sanctions are proposed.
   **Catastrophic loss:** yes, fish stocks can collapse.

6. **Yossarian’s missions — “MANY CONTEXTS, ONE CONCEPT”**  
   Opening: “the Second World War is almost won.” Yossarian and
   other service members face whether to keep risking their lives.
   Yossarian says he does not want to be “among the last to die.”
   **Family:** prisoners’ dilemma, explicitly called a
   “multiperson prisoners’ dilemma” later. His private incentive
   is to stop risking death; widespread refusal makes sustaining
   the war difficult. **Rule:** whether “everyone felt that way”
   changes the collective result; the text gives no numerical
   threshold or resolution. **Catastrophic loss:** yes, death.

11. **Axelrod tournaments — “TIT FOR TAT”**  
    Opening: “Robert Axelrod invited game theorists.” Computer
    strategies play the two-player dilemma **150 times** per
    pairing; total scores determine tournament rank. Stage-game
    numbers are not given. **Family:** repeated prisoners’
    dilemma. Tit for tat starts cooperative and copies the last
    opponent move; it won both tournaments. **Rule:** “Axelrod did
    not score the pairwise plays as winner-take-all”: under
    winner-take-all, tit for tat could not have had the best record.
    With mistakes, “more generous strategies” did better.
    **Catastrophic loss:** no literal destruction is specified.

12. **Noisy tit for tat — “TIT FOR TAT”**

    Strict tit for tat can perpetuate reprisals; generous
    strategies performed better in later noisy tournaments.
    **Rule:** “any mistake ‘echoes’ back and forth.”
    **Catastrophic loss:** no in the abstract example; the text
    compares the cycle with violent real feuds.

    **Catastrophic loss:** yes, the entire accumulated fund can be
    lost to both; no personal ruin is stated.

15. **Flood–Dresher 100-round experiment — “MORE RECENT

    “reciprocator” can support earlier cooperation.
    **Catastrophic loss:** no.

17. **Texas A&M production game — “MORE RECENT EXPERIMENTS”**  
    Opening: “Professor Raymond Battalio … had his class of 27
    students play.” All **27** independently choose output 1 or 2.
    All choosing 1 yields **$1.08 each**; all choosing 2 yields
    **$0.50 each**. With 26 choosing 1 and one choosing 2, the
    former get **$1.04** and the latter **$1.54**. Each switch
    raises the switcher’s payoff **$0.46** and reduces each
    colleague’s by **$0.04**. **Table not in text.**
    **Family:** N-player prisoners’ dilemma. Writing 2 is
    dominant. In the final binding play, four wrote 1 and the
    total paid was **$15.82**. **Rule:** if cheaters can be
    identified, “social sanctions such as ostracism” could deter
    them. **Catastrophic loss:** no.

    a south hunter facing north receives **½** after envy.
    **Table not in text.** **Family:** chicken. The two
    equilibria have one north and one south. **Rule:** a credible
    commitment to north, communicated to the other, can select who
    yields; alternating or a coin toss can compromise.
    **Catastrophic loss:** no; both north yields no meat.

51. **Cliff chicken — “A LITTLE HISTORY”**  
    Opening: “two teenagers drive their cars in parallel toward a
    cliff.” Each chooses when to jump; the first is chicken.
    Numerical payoffs and the exact crash outcome are not stated.
    **Family:** chicken / brinkmanship metaphor. The passage
    identifies the game as a metaphor for nuclear brinkmanship but
    defers further analysis. **Rule:** “the one who first jumps
    out of his car is the chicken.” **Catastrophic loss:** yes,
    driving toward a cliff creates a potential fatal crash, though
    this passage does not detail the result.

52. **Experimental trading markets — “A BEAUTIFUL EQUILIBRIUM?”**  
    Opening: “the markets set up by Vernon Smith.” Small numbers
    of traders exchange without direct knowledge of others’ costs
    or values. Specific bids and payoffs are not given.
    **Family:** market / other. The text reports rapid equilibrium
    exchanges. **Rule:** traders had “no direct knowledge of the
    others’ costs or values”; no contrasting treatment is stated.
    **Catastrophic loss:** not stated.

53. **Ultimatum game — “A BEAUTIFUL EQUILIBRIUM?”**  
    Opening: “one player makes a take-it-or-leave-it offer.”
    Two players sequentially divide a sum: one offers, the other
    accepts or rejects. The amount and rejection payoff are not
    given here. **Family:** bargaining. Observed offers were
    “surprisingly generous”; the authors point to
    other-regarding preferences. **Rule:** preferences beyond
    “purely selfish ones” affect the prediction. **Catastrophic
    loss:** no total loss is specified in this passage.

54. **Half Way, two players — “CASE STUDY: HALF WAY”**  
    Opening: “Each player is to pick a number between 0 and 100.”
    Two players privately choose numbers; the one closest to half
    the other’s number wins **$100**, split on a tie. **Family:**
    other / iterative reasoning. Both choosing **0** is Nash; 0
    beats any positive opposing number. The authors first chose
    **12.5** to illustrate limited reasoning, then chose 0.
    **Rule:** “having only two players” makes 0 always win or
    tie; that property fails in the N-player version.
    **Catastrophic loss:** no; a player can lose the prize.

55. **Half the average, N players — “CASE STUDY: HALF WAY”**  
    Opening: “Let’s modify the game to add more players.”
    N players choose numbers; the one closest to half the average
    wins. The passage does not restate the tie rule or prize for
    this variant. **Family:** other / iterative reasoning.
    All choosing **0** is the stated unique equilibrium, but 0
    does not always win. People rarely picked 0 initially and
    approached equilibrium after two or three plays.
    **Rule:** the target is “half the average number,” rather
    than half the other player’s number. **Catastrophic loss:** no
    loss beyond the prize is described.

56. **Dropped-call callback — “CASE STUDY: HALF WAY”**  
    Opening: “what to do when a mobile phone call gets dropped.”
    Two callers independently call or wait. Both waiting leaves
    no connection; simultaneous calls could produce busy signals.
    **Family:** coordination / chicken-like anti-coordination.
    The two equilibria are one calls while the other waits.
    **Rule:** a convention that “the person who first made the
    call” calls back selects one equilibrium. **Catastrophic
    loss:** no.

## Prisoners’ dilemma instances

The following cross-index includes examples the text explicitly
calls dilemmas and settings it presents as that structure. Game
numbers point to the fuller entries above.

- **Confessing prisoners, guilty or innocent — 1:** “each prisoner’s
  temptation to confess exists” in either case. Retaliation after
  release can deter a witness; the text does not give a numerical
  punishment threshold.
- **Gas stations and supermarkets — 2–3:** “They are all instances
  of the prisoners’ dilemma.” Rewards or punishments can alter
  incentives; no specific resolution is given for these stores.
- **Party platforms — 4:** included in the same explicit list.
  Swing voters make moving toward the center attractive; no
  resolution is supplied.
- **Fisheries — 5:** “any single fisherman would be a fool to try
  single-handed conservation” when others fish aggressively.
  Quotas require detection and enforcement.
- **Yossarian and the war — 6:** “a multiperson prisoners’ dilemma.”
  Widespread refusal compounds the collective problem; no solution
  is given.
- **Two-price catalog game — 7:** “both players use their dominant
  strategies” and both do worse than under credible joint
  cooperation. Rewards, credible agreements or punishment can
  change incentives.
- **Axelrod, noisy tit for tat, and tamarins — 11–13:** the
  “prisoners’ dilemma game repeated 150 times” rewards strategies
  differently under total-score versus winner-take-all scoring.
  Errors worsen strict tit for tat; greater forgiveness helps.
- **One-shot experiments and Friend or Foe — 14:** “almost half of
  the players choose the cooperative action.” Previous broadcast
  results changed later observed cooperation.
- **Changing partners and 100-round play — 15–16:** with exactly
  100 rounds, “defection cannot be punished in any future rounds”
  after the last one. Uncertainty about reciprocating types can
  support earlier cooperation.
- **27-firm classroom game — 17:** “Writing 2 is a dominant
  strategy.” Identifying cheaters could permit social sanctions.
- **Contribution and punishment games — 18–19:** “contributing
  nothing is the dominant strategy” in the four-player example.
  Revealed contributions and costly punishment increased
  contributions experimentally.
- **Flood control and conservation — 20:** each may “shirk or
  withhold his contribution” while benefiting from others.
  The passage supplies no specific village resolution.
- **Repeated and multiproduct catalog pricing — 21–22:** a
  “collapse of trust” creates future losses. Interest, continuation
  probability and product differences affect deterrence.
- **Information experiment — 23:** cooperation was higher when
  the other’s choice was undisclosed. The authors call the
  associated reasoning “quasi-magical.”
- **Business cartels and price-matching devices — 24–29:** “each
  firm can do better for itself by cheating.” Communication,
  visible bidding, faster detection and automatic retaliation can
  sustain collusion; law enforcement can disrupt it.
- **Commons and global warming — 30:** “This is just a multiperson
  prisoners’ dilemma.” Local rules, monitoring and graduated
  sanctions help, although “the dilemma never fully disappears.”
- **Vampire-bat reciprocal sharing — 31:** presented under
  “prisoners’ dilemmas arise in species other than humans.”
  Recognition and past assistance support reciprocal sharing.
- **Expanded pricing — 35–37:** the text says the several-price
  game “shares this feature” of the dilemma: credible collusion
  can improve both firms’ profits.
- **Rousseau’s hare interpretation — 33:** a later passage says
  Rousseau’s wording “seems to imply” a dominant hare strategy,
  making it a prisoners’ dilemma. The more common interpretation
  is an assurance game, where hunters prefer stag if others join.
- **Generic laboratory dilemmas — 52–53 context:** “good behavior
  occurred far more frequently than theory might lead people to
  believe.” The authors suggest other-regarding preferences as
  part of the explanation; no additional distinct payoff table is
  provided.

## Annihilation and escalation

- **Retaliatory feuds — game 12.** Quote: “any mistake ‘echoes’
  back and forth.” With strict tit for tat, one actual or
  perceived defection can produce continuing reprisals. The text
  reports that more generous strategies performed better when
  mistakes were allowed; it cites violent communal and family
  feuds as analogies.
- **Extreme nuclear punishment — game 21 context.** Quote: the WTO
  could “nuke any nation” breaking tariff commitments. This is a
  deliberately extreme hypothetical, not an observed incident.
  The text’s rule is that errors are possible, so punishment should
  be “as low as is compatible with successful deterrence.”
- **Finch population collapse — game 32.** Quote: “no nectar, no
  pollen, no seeds, no fruit, and then no more cactus finch.”
  A rare destructive adaptation may hurt its own local carriers;
  if pervasive, early access may become advantageous during the
  famine. The text describes population elimination and possible
  later recolonization, not a chosen preventive equilibrium.
- **Road chicken — game 49.** Quote: “If both keep straight …
  they crash.” Each prefers the other to yield; one yielding is
  an equilibrium. A credible commitment can select who yields.
- **Cliff chicken and nuclear brinkmanship — game 51.** Quote:
  “the one who first jumps out of his car is the chicken.” The
  text says Bertrand Russell and others used this as a metaphor
  for nuclear brinkmanship and defers the analysis to chapter 6.
  It gives no nuclear payoff table or best move in this passage.
- **Bargaining breakdown — game 44.** Quote: “both may suffer
  serious damage” if negotiations fail. A mutually recognized
  focal split can avoid disagreement; the passage does not say
  that either side is annihilated.
- **Escalating CEO pay — game 45.** Quote: “Every firm pays its
  CEO above last year’s average.” The comparison rule drives
  escalating salaries in the authors’ account. They suggest a
  different prestige focal point, without a formal equilibrium.
- **Auction escalation:** the file describes collusive turbine
  bids and spectrum-auction signals, but does **not** describe an
  escalating all-pay auction or provide its best move.
- **Doomsday devices and duels:** no doomsday-device or duel game
  appears in this file. Nuclear brinkmanship is mentioned only as
  the cliff-chicken metaphor and deferred discussion above.

## Doubts

- The file runs from **chapter 3 through the epilogue to part I**;
  it refers to chapters 1, 2, 6, 7, 10 and 14 without including
  those chapters. Their referenced games and answers cannot be
  reconstructed from this file alone.
- Several payoff tables and a graph are absent from the text.
  I have marked the affected entries **“table not in text”** and
  reported only numbers stated in the prose.
- Trip to the Gym no. 3 asks for an equilibrium but gives no
  workout answer in this file.
- “Can a player be eliminated or suffer catastrophic loss?” is
  sometimes underdetermined: several real-world examples describe
  dangerous settings without specifying the outcome for an
  individual player. Those entries say so rather than assigning a
  numerical loss.
- The book’s finch case expressly distinguishes its game from a
  classic prisoners’ dilemma. Rousseau’s wording, however, is
  described as admitting both a dilemma reading and the more
  common assurance-game reading.

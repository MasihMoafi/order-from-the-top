### Chapter 8: Interpreting and Manipulating Information

1. **Sue’s tattoo — “THE MARRYING KIND?”**  
   Opening: “Our friend, whom we’ll call Sue, was in love.” Two
   partners; she asks for a tattoo, then he accepts or refuses and she
   decides whether to wait. Sequential; payoffs are described as
   commitment versus an “embarrassing artifact.” **Signalling.**
   He “balked, so Sue left”; she later married someone else.
   **Changing rule:** the tattoo must cost the uncommitted type more:
   “if commitment wasn’t part of his plan,” it would embarrass him
   with a later partner. Catastrophe: no.

2. **Meal order and recommendation — “Tell It Like It Is?”**  
   Opening: “When you order a steak medium rare.” Two: customer and
   waiter; the customer states an order or requests advice, and the
   waiter responds. Sequential; the waiter wants to please the
   customer but a pricier recommendation may increase the tip.
   **Signalling.** Truthful ordering is best when interests align;
   the text says a cheaper recommendation is more credible.
   **Changing rule:** the waiter “might want to steer you to a more
   expensive item.” Catastrophe: no.

3. **Penalty-kick announcement — “Tell It Like It Is?”**  
   Opening: “Recall the soccer penalty kicker and the goalie.”
   Two: kicker announces right, then kicker and goalie choose
   directions. The underlying kick’s payoff table is not reproduced.
   **Zero-sum mixing.** Ignore the announcement and play the
   equilibrium of the underlying game. **Changing rule:** their
   “interests are totally opposed,” so neither the statement nor
   its opposite is reliable. Catastrophe: no.

4. **Solomon and the two claimants — “KING SOLOMON’S DILEMMA”**  
   Opening: “Two women came before King Solomon.” Three: two
   claimants respond to the king’s proposed division of the child;
   the king rules. Sequential; the true mother wants the child alive.
   **Signalling.** The false claimant should repeat the first
   woman’s answer; in the story she instead says to cut the child,
   and Solomon awards the living child to the other woman.
   **Changing rule:** the device “worked only because of the
   second woman’s error.” Catastrophe: **yes**—the threatened
   killing of the child.

5. **Law-firm hospitality — “DEVICES FOR MANIPULATING INFORMATION”**

   prospective interns, potentially many; the firm spends on
   hospitality, then interns infer how they will be treated.
   Sequential; the cost of hospitality is the stated sacrifice.
   **Signalling.** Lavish spending can convey that the firm values
   them. **Changing rule:** “if we valued you less then we would not
   find it in our interest to spend so much money.” Catastrophe: no.

6. **Credentials rat race — “DEVICES FOR MANIPULATING INFORMATION”**

   Catastrophe: no.

### Chapter 9: Cooperation and Coordination

26. **Ivy League football — “FOR WHOM THE BELL CURVE

### Chapter 10: Auctions, Bidding, and Contests

43. **Competing house auctions — chapter introduction**  
    Opening: “Imagine that you are interested in a house.”
    Buyer and other bidders face two auctions a week apart; buyer
    chooses whether to bid now or wait for the preferred house.
    **Auction.** The text poses the dilemma and gives no numerical
    solution. **Changing rule:** waiting risks “ending up with
    neither.” Catastrophe: no.

44. **English and Japanese auctions — “ENGLISH AND JAPANESE
    AUCTIONS”**  
    Opening: “The most famous type of auction is known as the
    English or ascending auction.” One seller, N bidders; ascending
    bids. In the Japanese version all signal participation and cannot
    reenter after dropping out. **Auction.** With private values and
    small increments, bid until the price reaches one’s value; the
    highest-value bidder wins at approximately the second-highest
    value. **Changing rule:** Japanese dropout reveals who remains;
    in common-value settings that information can change valuation.
    Catastrophe: no.

45. **Vickrey auction and Gym 6 — “VICKREY AUCTION” /
    “TRIP TO THE GYM NO. 6”**  
    Opening: “In 1961, Columbia University economist.”
    N bidders submit sealed bids simultaneously; highest wins and
    pays the second-highest bid. **Auction.** With private values,
    truthful bidding is dominant. Example: value **$60**, a bid of
    **$50** loses unnecessarily against **$53**; a $60 bid wins
    and pays $53. Gym 6 asks about advance knowledge of rivals’
    bids; the text provides no separate answer. **Changing rule:**
    payment is the second-highest bid, not the winner’s bid.
    Catastrophe: no.

46. **Buyer’s premium — “Buyer’s Premium”**  
    Opening: “If you win an auction at Sotheby’s or Christie’s.”
    Seller, auction house, and N bidders; bidders account for a

    to pay **$600** bids up to **$500**; the text says the seller
    bears the premium through lower bids. **Changing rule:** the
    buyer must be “aware of this rule and take it into account.”
    Catastrophe: no.

47. **eBay proxy bidding and sniping — “ONLINE AUCTIONS” /
    “Sniping”**

    **Changing rule:** early bids reveal information and permit
    responses; simultaneous, once-only proxies would have the
    Vickrey property. Catastrophe: no.

48. **ACME acquisition — “ACME”**

    Conditional on acceptance, **$6m** is the break-even cap:

    acceptance reveals the value is below the bid. Catastrophe:

    **$4m** company produces a **$4m** loss.

    **Changing rule:** first-price payment makes truthful bidding
    unattractive; the quoted numbers depend on the symmetric
    private-value distribution. Catastrophe: no.

50. **Treasury bills — “T-BILLS”**  
    Opening: “Each week, the U.S. Treasury holds an auction.”
    Treasury and multiple bidders submit rates for quantities;

    **3.72%** win; under uniform pricing all winners receive

## Annihilation and escalation

- **Solomon’s child:** “Cut the living child in two.” The false
  claimant’s ability to copy the true mother’s response changes
  whether the test reveals the truth; the authors say she should
  repeat the first woman. The story ends with the child alive.
- **Wartime deception:** Marwan’s warning was discounted and
  the subsequent attack “almost overran the Israeli army.”
  The authors’ general advice is that, against a person trying
  to mislead, it may be best to ignore the statement. The file
  leaves Marwan’s allegiance uncertain.
- **Reactor choice:** the text says alternative reactors have a
  lower “risk of a meltdown.” Early adoption and learning can lock
  in a technology; it recommends considering future lock-in early.
  It does not describe an actual meltdown in this example.
- **Launch race:** “Pull too early and you’ll miss. Wait too long
  and you’ll get beaten.” A first mover can capture a one-firm
  market but may fail by launching before it is ready. Newton
  was scrapped; USA Today survived more than $1 billion in losses.
- **Duel:** “If you fire too soon and miss, your rival will be able
  to advance and hit with certainty.” Under the stated accuracy and
  payoff assumptions, both choose the first time when
  \(p(t)+q(t)=1\).
- **War of attrition:** “both firms were in danger of going under.”
  The longer-lasting firm wins, but both incur costs. When the rival
  is about to quit, the text says stay another period; because
  beliefs can produce massive losses, it recommends a deal.
  BSB and Murdoch merged.
- **Spectrum escalation:** to deny MCI both licences, AT&T must
  be prepared to bid a combined **17**. The authors prefer
  accepting one licence cheaply because contesting the second
  can raise the price of both.

This part contains **no developed chicken, brinkmanship, or

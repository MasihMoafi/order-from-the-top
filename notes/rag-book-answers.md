### How does brinkmanship work, and when does the risk of mutual destruction make a player back down or hold on?
Brinkmanship works by deliberately creating a risk of mutual disaster rather than making a flat threat. The threatener controls the size of the risk beforehand but not the outcome—like spinning the chamber in Russian roulette—so the opponent faces a growing chance that the bad outcome will occur. This is “chicken in real time”: the longer both sides keep going, the greater the risk, and the choice is not whether to swerve but when.

The risk of mutual destruction makes a player back down when the probability of the bad outcome becomes too large for its own tolerance before the other side’s limit is reached. The side with the lower tolerance for risk, or the one that fears a breakdown less, is the stronger party and can use brinkmanship as a weapon; the weaker side, which would “blink first,” is better advised not to embark on it. A player holds on by accepting more risk or by showing it can endure escalating losses longer, proving its determination and lower cost of waiting. Only when one side discovers the other is truly stronger does it decide to back down.

### Explain the duel, the three-way duel, the preemption game and the war of attrition, and their optimal strategies
A duel is a contest in which each side chooses when to fire, trading off a lower chance of hitting early against the risk of being shot first. The optimal strategy is to wait until the point where your probability of hitting equals your rival's chance of missing—equivalently, until the two chances of success sum to 1. Both players then want to fire at the same time, even if their abilities differ, and the accuracy of the pistols does not change the survival probability, because players adjust how close they get before firing.

A three-way duel has three antagonists (Larry, Moe, and Curly) taking turns in two rounds, with the best outcome being sole survivor and the worst being killed. The optimal strategy for the weakest shot, Larry, is to deliberately miss—fire into the air—so the stronger players target each other. This turns his 30 percent accuracy into about a 41.2 percent chance of winning, while the most accurate player, Curly, has the lowest survival chance at only 14 percent.

A preemption game is the opposite of waiting: the first to launch or act can own the market, but acting too early risks failure and waiting too long risks being beaten. It can be modeled as an auction where the bid is the time to act, and the optimal strategy is to fire at the moment your chance of success equals your rival's chance of failure—the same condition as the duel.

A war of attrition is the reverse of preemption: the objective is to outlast your rival rather than to go first. It is like an auction in which all participants pay their bid (measured in losses endured) and the longest-lasting bidder wins. There is no single best bidding strategy; if you believe the other side is about to fold, you should stay in another period, and your belief about their willingness to continue depends on what they think you will do.

### What makes cooperation succeed: detection, punishment, clarity, certainty, size, repetition, and group composition
Cooperation is more likely to succeed when cheating can be detected quickly and accurately, when punishments are clear, credible, and appropriately sized, when the game is repeated so future losses deter short-term cheating, and when the group has clear rules about membership and permissible behavior. Detection should ideally be automatic or built into normal routines, and in groups of three or more it must identify who cheated so punishment can be targeted. Punishment works best when it is clear and understood, certain to follow defection, and graduated rather than excessively harsh, especially because mistakes are possible. Repetition matters because the long-run cost of lost cooperation can outweigh a one-time gain, with low interest rates making the future more valuable. Group composition and communication also help: regular meetings and shared norms make acceptable behavior and cheating clearer, while stable communities can enforce sanctions through social ostracism or other credible penalties.
